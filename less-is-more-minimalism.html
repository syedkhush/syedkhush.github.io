Less, But Which Less?

Minimalism isn't about owning less. It's about knowing what everything is for.

syed sami  ·  October 2026  ·  3 min read

There's a shelf in my room I keep meaning to clear. Old chargers, a notebook I never finished, a cable for a device I no longer own. Every few weeks I look at it and think: this is clutter.

Then I pick up the notebook, and I'm back in the month I started it. I put it down again.

That small moment has taught me more about minimalism than any list of rules.

The phrase

"Less is more" is usually credited to the architect Mies van der Rohe, though the words are older than his buildings. It sounds like a slogan, and slogans are easy to nod along to. But taken literally it's false. Less is not more. Less is less.

What the phrase really says is that after you remove everything that doesn't matter, what's left matters more. The subtraction isn't the point. The attention you get back is.

What the clutter costs

Every object you own asks for something small. A glance, a decision, a place to be kept, a thought when it breaks. One of them costs nothing you'd notice. A hundred of them add up to a low hum in the background of your day.

"Attention is the one thing you can't buy more of, and things quietly charge you for it."

I think this is the real argument for minimalism. It isn't about looking tidy. It's about what the mess takes from you without asking.

Where it goes wrong

But there's a trap, and I've seen it. Minimalism can turn into a competition: who owns the fewest things, who has the emptiest room. At that point it's not freedom, it's decoration. You've swapped one kind of clutter for another, and the new kind is a performance.

And some clutter isn't clutter at all. My notebook holds nothing useful. It holds a month of my life. If I threw it out to win an argument about shelves, I'd have a cleaner room and a thinner past.

The question I ask now

I stopped asking do I need this? because the honest answer is almost always "not really." I ask a different one:

What is it for?

A phone is for reaching people. A chair is for rest. The notebook is for remembering. If I can answer that, I keep it. If I can't, it's probably just taking up space, and I let it go without guilt.

One less thing

This isn't only about objects. It works on habits, on commitments, even on opinions. Most of us are carrying things we picked up years ago and never decided to keep.

So here's something small to try. Pick one thing today, an object, a subscription, a thing you say yes to out of habit, and ask what it's for. If you have an answer, good. If not, you've just found your first less.

What's the one thing you'd keep, even though nobody else would understand why? Tell me in the comments.
